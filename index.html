In brief

Two days, one city, one question

If every competitor runs on the same AI, what makes yours worth choosing? DDX’26 Miami put that question to nineteen speakers from Google DeepMind, Medium, Adobe, Amazon, Docusign, American Express, Universal Destinations & Experiences, Brain Technologies, Askable, Citi and Miami’s own design community, and to a room that answered back at every table.

The day opened on Thursday evening at the University of Miami, where three design leaders unlearned leadership in disruptive times. It continued on Friday at The LAB Miami: seven talks, two panels, two hands-on workshops and six table talks, closing with a keynote on the last screen and a room that stayed for the after-event.

The answer that emerged was consistent from the first slide to the last: AI makes everything personal, and personal isn’t human. When the models, the vendors and the metrics are the same, difference lives in judgment, in the tensions a team chooses to hold, and in the room a service leaves for a person to do the unscripted thing. Design’s job is moving upstream, closer to the decision.

Personal isn’t human.

Same models, same vendors, same data. Difference is the value.

Meghan Preiss opened the main stage with a man running through an airport in his socks and a pilot who waited twelve minutes: nobody designed that, somebody left room for it. Her argument ran through the whole day: AI makes every service individually better and collectively the same, personalisation is a mail merge with better manners, and when sameness is cheap, difference is the value. Difference lives in interaction, in the tensions a company chooses to hold rather than resolve toward the metric, and in the emotions no script asked for. Let the machines be personal. Let people be human.

Feel it. Design belongs closer to the decision.

Cassie McDaniel’s talk gave the room permission it did not know it needed: you can make decisions without data. There are three sources of information that lead to product decisions, feels, reason and data, and a design team that only brings the third one is not in the room where the call is made. Monica Girel showed what that looks like in practice: information architecture still creates enormous value, but a static IA map no longer works for non-deterministic experiences, so she mapped a new framework for AI features that feel integrated with the product they live in. On the agentic-design panel with Gleb Kuznetsov, moderated by Sebastian Gier, the same point came back as a leadership question: who is allowed to say yes.

Six months with an agent: detach, focus, align, delegate, audit.

Sures Kumar handed an AI agent his calendar, fifteen years of email, ten years of transactions, his journals and his lab results, and reported back. Some of it worked better than expected; some of it he took back, and every failure fell into one of three buckets: the agent was missing context it was never given, the information could not be captured, or it had everything and still reasoned badly. His five-step process for using agents safely, at work and at home, was the most photographed slide of the day. Gleb Kuznetsov closed the stage with the harder version of the question: the last thing you shipped, what does it do when nobody is interacting with it?

The best design system is one AI can’t forget to use.

Ivan Barriga’s workshop introduced the Design Genome: design systems evolving from passive documentation into living infrastructure that both humans and AI can read, so generative tools inherit a company’s design language by default instead of being told the rules with every prompt. Jonathan Ruiz, who runs the team behind Ink at Docusign, framed the same shift as a management problem: the future of design is less about the tools than the systems that keep AI-assisted work consistent, coherent and human at scale. Dianne Alter called the designers who will win the ones who embrace orchestration, feeding the right context and taste into an AI to build the right thing instead of the generic thing.

Research at the scale of the business, judgment in the loop.

Elizabeth Reme is the sole researcher across American Express’s customer-service ecosystem, and her workshop walked through the GenAI-assisted synthesis pipeline she built to process more than 200,000 open-ended responses a quarter at zero extra tooling cost. Attendees sorted a sample by hand, compared their groupings with an AI pass, and found the costly failure mode: feedback that sounds identical but points to different root causes. Liz Steelman’s MCP roundtable turned tool-connected research queries into a card game; Coral Miniel’s table asked how to keep human judgment at the centre once prototypes arrive faster than understanding. The closing panel, More Human, Not Less, gave the day its verdict.
